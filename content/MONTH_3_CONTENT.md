## FACEBOOK POST #17
### Long-Form: Money Conversations

**Title:** Money Conversations with Your Co-Parent: Scripts That Work

**Content:**
```
Money and co-parenting: two topics that, combined, create some of the most heated conflicts.

Here's how to handle financial conversations without them becoming wars.

**WHY MONEY IS SO HARD**

Money conversations trigger conflict because they're rarely just about money. They carry:
• Resentment from the relationship
• Different values and priorities
• Power and control dynamics
• Guilt about what kids "need"
• Disagreement about what's "fair"

Knowing this helps: When a simple expense request turns into a fight, it's usually about more than the money.

**THE BIFF APPROACH TO EXPENSES**

**Brief:**
"Emma needs soccer cleats. They're $85. Can you cover half?"

Not: "Emma REALLY needs these cleats because her old ones don't fit and I've already paid for her uniform and registration and I think it's only fair that you contribute since you never help with these things..."

**Informative:**
Include the facts. Cost. Deadline. How to split. Receipt or link if possible.

"Soccer cleats: $85
Needed by: Saturday for first game
Request: $42.50 each
[Link to cleats]"

**Friendly:**
Neutral tone. Not a demand. Not a guilt trip.

"Let me know if you can contribute by Friday."

**Firm:**
That's the end of the conversation. No follow-up guilt. No "well, if you CAN'T, I guess I'll have to..." Just the request.

**WHEN THEY SAY NO**

They might decline. They might ignore you. They might counter with their own request.

Your options:
• Cover it yourself (if you choose to)
• Wait for what the parenting agreement specifies
• Document the conversation for future reference
• Adjust expectations for future requests

What you don't have to do: Fight about it.

**WHEN YOU NEED TO SAY NO**

The same principles apply when they request money from you:

• You can say no without explaining your finances
• You can offer a partial contribution
• You can request documentation/receipts
• You can refer to the parenting agreement

"I'm not able to contribute to that expense. Let me know if there's anything else."

**BIGGER FINANCIAL CONFLICTS**

Some financial issues are bigger than cleats:
• Child support modifications
• Major expenses (braces, tutoring, therapy)
• Disagreements about what the agreement covers

For these, the advice is the same: Document everything. Communicate in writing. Consult your attorney when needed.

But day-to-day expense conversations? BIFF. Facts. Done.

## FACEBOOK POST #19
### Long-Form: Self-Awareness

**Title:** When You're Contributing to the Conflict: Honest Self-Assessment

**Content:**
```
This is the uncomfortable post.

Most co-parenting content focuses (rightly) on protecting yourself from difficult behavior. Boundaries. BIFF. Parallel parenting. All important.

But sometimes the honest question is: "Am I ever making this harder?"

**THIS ISN'T ABOUT BLAME**

Let's be clear: This post isn't suggesting you're responsible for someone else's behavior. If you're dealing with genuine high-conflict communication, that's real. That's not your fault.

But self-awareness means being willing to examine your own patterns—not to excuse their behavior, but to find where YOU have power.

**QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING**

• Do I sometimes respond when silence would be more effective?
• Do I match their energy instead of choosing my own?
• Do I over-explain when one sentence (BIFF) would do?
• Do I rehearse arguments in my head between messages?
• Do I sometimes want to "win" more than I want to resolve?
• Have I avoided setting boundaries that would have helped?
• Do I vent to others in ways that keep me stuck in the conflict?

**BOTH THINGS CAN BE TRUE**

You can be dealing with genuinely difficult behavior AND have patterns worth examining.

Their behavior being a problem doesn't mean you're perfect. And noticing your patterns doesn't excuse what they do.

Both things can be true.

**WHAT SELF-AWARENESS LOOKS LIKE**

Self-awareness in co-parenting means:
• Noticing your patterns without judgment
• Taking responsibility for your part
• Changing what you CAN change
• NOT excusing their behavior
• NOT blaming yourself for everything
• Seeking growth, not perfection

**COMMON PATTERNS TO NOTICE**

Over-explaining: When one sentence would do, but you write five paragraphs hoping they'll finally understand.

Matching energy: When they're hostile and you respond with hostility. When they're passive-aggressive and you match it.

Seeking validation: When you need them to acknowledge you're right more than you need the conversation to end.

Avoiding boundaries: When you let things slide to "keep the peace" until you explode.

Staying in the conflict mentally: When you replay conversations, draft responses you'll never send, and carry the conflict even when you're not communicating.

**THE POWER IN NOTICING**

When you notice a pattern, you have a choice.

Next time they send a hostile message, you can notice: "My pattern is to match their energy. This time, I'm going to choose BIFF instead."

That's not weakness. That's self-leadership.

**A NOTE ON WHEN THIS DOESN'T APPLY**

If you're in a situation involving abuse, manipulation, or safety concerns, self-reflection is not the priority. Safety is.

This post is for situations where the conflict is difficult but not dangerous—and where examining your own patterns might help.

## FACEBOOK POST #23
### Long-Form: The Long Game

**Title:** The Long Game: Maintaining Your Mental Health Through 18+ Years of Co-Parenting

**Content:**
```
Here's a truth that can feel overwhelming or liberating, depending on how you look at it:

Co-parenting isn't a sprint. It's potentially 18+ years of communication. Maybe longer—there will be graduations, weddings, grandchildren.

That's the long game.

**WHY THIS PERSPECTIVE MATTERS**

When you're in the middle of a difficult exchange, it's easy to focus on winning. Being right. Making them understand.

But when you zoom out to the long view, the question changes:

Not: "How do I survive this week?"
But: "What serves me for 18 years?"

**WHAT DOESN'T MATTER LONG-TERM**

• Winning individual exchanges
• Being "right" in every conflict
• Making them finally understand you
• Getting them to change
• Perfect documentation of every slight
• Having the last word

**WHAT ACTUALLY MATTERS LONG-TERM**

• Your relationship with your kids
• Your mental and physical health
• Modeling healthy communication (your kids are watching)
• Protecting your peace sustainably
• Having energy for your life, not just the conflict

**SUSTAINABLE CO-PARENTING**

Sustainability means the strategies you use don't burn you out.

Sustainable looks like:
• BIFF as your default response (not exhausting effort)
• Boundaries that don't require daily fighting to maintain
• Parallel parenting if traditional co-parenting doesn't work
• Energy spent on your kids, not the conflict
• Acceptance of what you can't control

Unsustainable looks like:
• Fighting every battle
• Needing to be understood
• Trying to change them
• Hypervigilant documentation
• Constant mental rehearsal of conflicts

**THE BATTLES WORTH FIGHTING**

Not every issue deserves your energy. Ask:

• Does this affect my children's safety or wellbeing?
• Will this matter in a year? Five years?
• Is engaging going to change anything?
• Is the cost of fighting worth the potential outcome?

Some battles are worth it. Many aren't.

**YOUR KIDS ARE WATCHING**

This might be the most important long-game truth:

Your children are learning how to handle conflict by watching you. They're learning about boundaries, communication, and self-respect.

What they'll remember isn't who "won" the co-parenting battles. They'll remember:
• How present you were during your time together
• Whether you put them in the middle
• How you handled stress
• The peace (or chaos) in your home

Modeling healthy communication—even when it's hard—is a gift that lasts beyond 18 years.

**PROTECTING YOUR PEACE LONG-TERM**

This isn't selfish. It's sustainable.

• Building a support system (friends, therapy, community)
• Recognizing when you need breaks from the conflict mentally
• Celebrating progress, not just waiting for perfection
• Accepting that some things won't change—and focusing on what you can control

**THE LONG VIEW**

Eighteen years is a long time. It's also not forever.

Your kids will grow. The intensity will change. What feels overwhelming now won't always be this way.

Your job isn't to fix everything. It's to:
• Protect your peace
• Model healthy communication
• Be present for your kids
• Take care of yourself along the way

That's the long game. That's what matters.

—

This is educational content, not therapy.
```
